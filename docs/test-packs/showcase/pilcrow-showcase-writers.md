# The Lamplighter of Greywater Quay

> A lamp on a quay is not only a light. It is a mark, and a mark is a promise: *the harbour is this way.*

## Chapter One: The Last Lamp

The fog came in early that November, rolling over the breakwater like a slow grey tide, and by four o'clock Maren Holloway could no longer see the far end of the quay. She did not mind. She had lit the lamps of Greywater for thirty-one years, and she knew them the way a pianist knows a keyboard: by distance, by the weight of the pole in her hands, by the small differences in the way each wick caught.

There were forty-two lamps along the quay. The first stood outside the net loft, where the old stones were worn smooth by boots. The last stood at the very end of the pier, beyond the bench where nobody sat any more, and it was the one she liked least. It leaned a little to the east, as if it were listening for something out at sea.

*Forty-two,* she said to herself, as she did every evening. *Forty-two, and not one of them is mine.*

The town council had voted in the spring to replace the lamps with electric ones. The vote was close, and polite, and entirely final.[^1] The new posts would arrive after the winter storms, the clerk had said. There was no hurry. Nobody had said what would happen to the lamplighter, and Maren had not asked.

> A town does not remember the people who lit its streets. It remembers only that the streets were bright, and forgets to ask by whom.

She had found those words pencilled inside the lid of the oil locker on her first day, in a hand she did not recognise. Her predecessor had been a quiet man named Teodor Vane, and he had left her the key, the ladder, and the sentence.

## Chapter Two: A Visitor at the Pier

On the third night of the fog, Maren reached the last lamp and found someone sitting on the bench.

It was a girl, perhaps twelve years old, wrapped in a coat too large for her. She had a canvas bag at her feet and a look of great concentration, as though she were holding a very full cup and did not wish to spill it.

"You're late," the girl said.

"I am exactly on time," said Maren. "I have been on time for thirty-one years."

"Then I was early." The girl considered this. "My name is Ivy Marlow. I'm told you know about the boats."

Maren set down her pole. It was an unusual thing to be told. In Greywater, one knew about the boats the way one knew about the weather: everyone, loosely, and nobody for certain. But she had spent more nights on the quay than anyone alive, and the girl was right that the quay noticed things.

"What about the boats?"

"One of them is missing," said Ivy. "My uncle's. The *Sandpiper*. She went out on Tuesday and did not come back, and the harbour office says the fog is the reason, and that she'll turn up when it lifts." She looked out into the grey. "I don't think the fog is the reason."

Maren lit the last lamp. It caught on the second try, which was usual, and threw a soft yellow circle on the planks. Beyond it, the world ended.

"Sit a while," she said. "Tell me what you do think."

## Chapter Three: What the Lamps Remember

They talked for most of an hour, and by the end of it Maren had decided two things. The first was that Ivy Marlow was extremely clever. The second was that she herself was going to be in considerable trouble.

The *Sandpiper* had left the harbour at dawn on Tuesday, said Ivy, with her uncle and one deckhand aboard. She had been seen passing the breakwater at about half past six. She had not been seen after that. This was the whole of the official story.

But Ivy had a notebook, and in the notebook she had written down everything her uncle had told her in the weeks before, in the way a child writes down the things she has not quite understood.

"He kept saying the light was wrong," she said. "Not the weather. The *light*. He said one of the lamps on the quay had been behaving oddly, and that a boat steering by it would end up in the wrong place."

"Which lamp?"

Ivy turned the pages. "He called it *the one that listens*."

Maren did not say anything. She looked at the last lamp, leaning gently to the east, and felt the old familiar chill creep along the back of her neck.

There are some things a lamplighter learns slowly. One of them is that a lamp on a quay is not only a light. It is a mark, and a mark is a promise: *the harbour is this way.* Sailors steer by it without thinking, in the same way that a person walking in the dark trusts that the stairs are where they were yesterday.[^2] To move a lamp, even a few feet, is to tell every boat a small and very dangerous lie.

"Did anyone touch it?" Ivy asked.

"The council's surveyors were here in September," said Maren slowly. "They measured the posts for the new ones. They had a long pole and a good deal of equipment. I was told they would not disturb anything."

"Did they?"

"I don't know." She laid her hand on the cold iron. "But I think I had better find out."

## Chapter Four: The Measure of the Quay

She began at dawn, which was against all her habits, for the lamplighter's day starts when other people's ends.

The fog had thinned a little. It lay in long bands over the water now, silver on the top and dark underneath, and the quay emerged from it a piece at a time. Maren walked the whole length with a ball of string, a tape and the surveyors' map, which Ivy had somehow obtained from the clerk's office and would not explain.

The map showed forty-two lamps. Every one of them was marked with a small cross and a number. And every one of them, Maren found as she walked, was where the map said it should be.

Except the last.

The map put it eleven paces from the end of the pier, a little north of centre. The lamp itself stood at nine paces, and a good deal further east.

"Two paces," said Ivy, writing it down. "Is that enough to matter?"

"For a rowing boat, no." Maren straightened and looked out where the water was just beginning to show. "For a fishing boat coming in at night, with the tide behind her and the channel no wider than a street? It's the difference between the harbour and the rocks."

She said nothing more for a while. There was a sound at the edge of hearing, a small steady knocking, and it took her a moment to realise that it was her own heart.

### What the old man knew

Back in the net loft, among the coils of rope and the smell of tar, Maren opened the oil locker and took out Teodor Vane's logbook.

She had never read it all the way through. It was not that kind of book. It was a column of dates and a column of weather and, at the right, a short phrase for each night: *Lit at dusk. Wind SW. Lamp seven sluggish. Trimmed.* Thirty years of such entries, in a hand that grew smaller and neater as the pages went on.

But once a year, every spring, a different note appeared in the margin. It was always the same note.

*Last lamp paced. Eleven from the end. True.*

"He measured it," said Ivy, reading over her shoulder. "Every year. Why would anyone measure a lamp?"

"Because he did not trust it to stay put." Maren turned the pages faster. "And I never once did. Thirty-one years, and I walked to that lamp by habit, not by count."

## Chapter Five: The Quiet Hours

That night she did not light the lamps in order.

She started at the pier, with the last one, and she worked back along the quay, so that the whole length of Greywater came alive behind her instead of ahead. It was an odd feeling. Forty-two small flames, and each one steadier than the one before, as if the town were slowly waking up.

At the net loft she stopped. Someone was waiting by the door: a tall man in a council coat, holding his hat in both hands.

"Mrs Holloway," he said. "I understand you have been measuring."

"I understand you have been moving my lamp."

The man had the grace to look uncomfortable. He gave his name as Alder Quinn, assistant to the harbour master, and he spoke quickly, as one does when a prepared speech has failed in the first sentence. The lamp had been shifted in September, he said, to make room for a drainage trench. It was meant to be temporary. A note had been filed. The note, it appeared, had been filed in a drawer that nobody opened.

"And the *Sandpiper*?" said Ivy, who had appeared at Maren's elbow with her notebook already open.

Quinn looked at the girl, and at the lamp, and then at the grey water beyond it, and his face changed.

"Oh," he said quietly. "Oh, no."

> **A note on the lamps.** Every lamp on the quay is older than the oldest sailor who steers by it. Nobody has ever written down where they should stand. They stand where they have always stood, and that, until now, has been enough.

## Epilogue: Morning

The *Sandpiper* was found on the second day after the fog lifted, aground but whole on a shingle bank two miles up the coast, her crew cold, hungry and alive. Ivy's uncle was, by every account, furious, mostly at himself, and he spent a full afternoon shaking Maren's hand in the doorway of the net loft until she was forced to ask him to stop.

The last lamp was moved back that week, eleven paces from the end of the pier, a little north of centre, exactly where the map had said. Maren set it herself, with Ivy holding the tape and a small crowd watching from the quay, and nobody said very much.

The council voted again in the spring. This time the vote was not close.

The electric posts still came, in the end, and they were good ones, tall and clean and quite bright. But they were set between the old lamps, not in place of them, and every evening, at dusk, a woman with a long pole walked the length of Greywater Quay and lit forty-two small flames by hand.

*Forty-two,* she said to herself, as she always did. *And every one of them is mine.*

[^1]: The motion passed by seven votes to six. One councillor, who had grown up on the quay, left the room before the count and did not return.
[^2]: Teodor Vane's own phrase for this was "the contract of the dark": an agreement that no one signs and everyone keeps.
